The Proof of God in a Christening Ledger

VOCABULARY TREATMENT

Author's Note

It may be comforting to know that your doctor has proof of the existence of God, especially if he's a flagrant Scottish alcoholic.

In 1710, Scottish doctor John Arbuthnot presented a new proof for the existence of God.1 He had observed that for 82 years in a row, London counted more christenings1 of baby boys than girls. Assuming that the probability of birthing a girl is equal to that of birthing a boy, and that it varies independently over years, the odds of this outcome occurring by chance are 0.5^82. It follows that the ratio must be governed2 not by random chance but by a divine3 unifying principle4.

Pierre-Simon Laplace revisited the data in an analysis published in 1781, and concluded more soberly5 that the probability of birthing a boy is simply a bit higher than one in two. Further interested in the difference in male-to-female birth proportions6 in various European cities, Laplace found that this proportion was 0.38% higher in London than in Paris, which he found significant7.2 The same comparison between Paris and Naples yielded a probability of 1/100, which Laplace didn't consider "sufficiently extreme8 for an irrevocable9 pronouncement10."

These early tests of quantitative hypotheses11 illustrate both the risks and merits12 of observational evidence13. To be sure, as Arbuthnot showed, it's easy enough to find that the data proves something you wanted to be true all along. Yet it is remarkable14 that both Arbuthnot and Laplace could obtain local records and start doing science right from their desks. In doing so, both correctly documented15 important phenomena16 without the need to invest much labor or capital17 to get the data.

Vocabulary

↑ 1. Christenings: (KRIS-uh-nings) - religious ceremonies in which a baby is officially named and welcomed into the Christian church; baptisms, naming ceremonies. The church held christenings every Sunday afternoon.
↑ 2. Governed: (GUHV-urnd) - controlled or determined by a particular rule, system, or force; controlled, ruled. The company's pricing is governed by strict internal policy.
↑ 3. Divine: (dih-VYNE) - relating to or coming from God or a god; holy, sacred. Many ancient cultures saw natural disasters as divine punishment.
↑ 4. Unifying principle: (YOO-nuh-fy-ing PRIN-suh-pul) - a single idea or rule that connects and explains many different things; guiding rule, organizing concept. Gravity is the unifying principle behind planetary motion.
↑ 5. Soberly: (SOH-bur-lee) - in a serious, calm, and realistic way, without exaggeration; seriously, level-headedly. She soberly assessed the risks before signing the contract.
↑ 6. Proportions: (pruh-POR-shunz) - the relative sizes or amounts of parts within a whole; ratios, shares. The report broke down revenue proportions by region.
↑ 7. Significant: (sig-NIF-uh-kunt) - large or important enough to be noticed or to matter, especially in statistics; notable, meaningful. The survey found a significant difference between the two groups.
↑ 8. Sufficiently extreme: (suh-FISH-unt-lee ik-STREEM) - far enough beyond the normal range to be considered unusual or noteworthy; extreme enough, pronounced enough. The temperature drop wasn't sufficiently extreme to cancel the flight.
↑ 9. Irrevocable: (ih-REV-uh-kuh-bul) - unable to be changed, reversed, or undone; final, permanent. Once he signed the contract, the decision was irrevocable.
↑ 10. Pronouncement: (pruh-NOWNS-munt) - a formal or authoritative statement or judgment; declaration, verdict. The judge's pronouncement ended months of uncertainty.
↑ 11. Quantitative hypotheses: (KWAHN-tuh-tay-tiv hy-PAH-thuh-seez) - proposed explanations that can be tested using numbers and measurable data; numerical theories, data-based predictions. Researchers use quantitative hypotheses to test claims with statistics.
↑ 12. Merits: (MEH-rits) - the good qualities or advantages of something that deserve praise or attention; strengths, advantages. The team debated the merits of each proposal.
↑ 13. Observational evidence: (ahb-zur-VAY-shuh-nul EV-uh-duhns) - information gathered by watching and recording real-world events, rather than through controlled experiments; field data, real-world data. Observational evidence suggested a link between the two trends.
↑ 14. Remarkable: (rih-MAR-kuh-bul) - worthy of attention because unusual or impressive; striking, extraordinary. It's remarkable how quickly the technology has advanced.
↑ 15. Documented: (DAH-kyuh-men-tid) - recorded in detail, usually in writing, as proof that something happened; recorded, logged. The researchers documented every observation carefully.
↑ 16. Phenomena: (fuh-NAH-muh-nuh) - facts or events, especially unusual ones, that can be observed and studied; occurrences, events. Scientists study natural phenomena like lightning and earthquakes.
↑ 17. Capital: (KAP-uh-tul) - wealth, especially money, used to start or grow a business or project; funds, financial resources. The startup needed more capital to expand overseas.
